Review #906A
===========================================================================

Paper Summary
-------------
The paper considers privacy-preserving graph neural networks by revisiting existing results from the perspective of explainability. Firstly, the paper identifies a feature-free baseline, without sensitive information, against which to test existing methods, observing that privacy-preserving methods are no better than the feature-free baseline. The experimental results show that this holds for all values of epsilon tested, suggesting that sensitive features are not necessary for utility. Following this, the paper then explores explanations for this anomalous behaviour. By analysing the representations used in denoising, the paper shows that the representation matrix can be decomposed into a topology-specific component, feature-specific component and noise component. The analysis shows that the topology component dominates, and ablation studies confirm this. Next, the paper shows that the unbiased rectification step used for LDP affects the training of the GNN for downstream tasks, causing low utility. Again, experiments confirm this analysis. Finally, the paper looks at the state-of-the-art mechanism UPGNET, reinterpreting their results in light of the aforementioned theoretical analysis, which is again confirmed experimentally.

Review #906B
===========================================================================

Paper Summary
-------------
This paper considers graphs where the structure (the adjacency matrix) is known to the server, while the features attached to the nodes are private sensitive information to be protected. It argues that, in this context,  existing evaluations of locally differentially private graph neural networks (LDPGNNs) are missing an essential comparison: a "feature-free" baseline that trains a GNN using only the graph's topology, disregarding the node features. The paper tests four representative LDPGNN methods (LPGNN, PrivGE, and two UPGNET variants) against this baseline across four real-world datasets, three GNN architectures, and a wide range of privacy budgets. The paper finds that none of the LDPGNN methods consistently outperforms the feature-free baseline: the maximum accuracy gain observed is under 1%, and is sometimes negative. The paper then explains this result theoretically and empirically: it decomposes the representations LDPGNNs produce into a topology-only term, a raw-feature term, and a noise term, and shows the topology term dominates, meaning the "denoising" step in LDPGNN pipelines mainly re-encodes graph structure rather than recovering meaningful private feature information.

The paper further investigates why LDPGNN utility still drops at low privacy budgets, despite this topology-dominance finding, which would seem to predict stability. It attributes this drop to the unbiased rectification step used by standard LDP mechanisms, which introduces a large scale-correction multiplier at low privacy budgets; this multiplier inflates the numerical magnitude of inputs fed to the GNN, harming trainability rather than destroying useful information. The authors show that this utility drop can be substantially remedied simply by removing or rescaling this multiplier. Finally, they use these two insights to reinterpret the state-of-the-art method UPGNET, arguing that its two key components work not by better estimating raw features (the original explanation) but by better exploiting topology and controlling input scale, respectively. The paper concludes by recommending that future LDPGNN research adopt feature-free baselines and rethink the standard "perturb–rectify–denoise" pipeline design.

Technical Correctness
---------------------
3. Fixable Major Issues

Technical Correctness Comments
------------------------------
Major Issues: 

1. All four datasets used (Cora, CiteSeer, LastFM, Facebook) are graphs where topology alone is already known to work well. Cora and CiteSeer are well-documented as strongly homophilous: The standard homophily ratio metric used to quantify this (Zhu et al., NeurIPS 2020) has been reported at approximately 0.79–0.81 for Cora and 0.74 for CiteSeer in independent studies (e.g., arXiv:2411.17296, arXiv:2407.01635). Homophily is understood in the GNN literature as strongly predictive of how much topology alone can contribute to classification accuracy, and this relationship is directly demonstrated on datasets closely related to this paper's setting: the paper introducing the LastFM dataset used in this study (Rozemberczki & Sarkar, CIKM 2020) reports that a purely structural embedding method (FEATHER) already matches or slightly outperforms full-feature GNNs on Facebook and LastFM (by 0.1–2.1% test AUC). On the other hand, the paper under review presents its finding (i.e., that LDPGNN methods fail to outperform a topology-only baseline) without noting that this comparison is likely to be easiest to demonstrate on strongly homophilous graphs, where topology alone is already known to be highly predictive. In addition, the paper states this finding in general terms, applicable to LDPGNN research as a whole, without checking whether it would still hold on non-homophilous graphs. While the framing of the finding's significance is arguably a presentation issue, the unjustified generality of the claim is, in my opinion, a major technical flaw.

2.  The feature-free baseline's random feature dimension is tuned over a wide range, up to several times the real feature size of the datasets, while the competing LDPGNN methods are run only with the settings recommended in their original papers, without the same tuning effort. Giving the baseline more capacity and more tuning could favor it for reasons unrelated to the topology-vs-features issue.

Minor Issues: 

3. Theorem 1 relies on a property specific to the multi-bit mechanism (MBM): its reports always have the same fixed size. This property does not hold for the other two mechanisms tested (PM, HDS), so the same proof likely doesn't carry over directly, and the same issue applies to the explanation of why NFR works (Section 5.5.2). For PM and HDS, the paper instead relies on weaker empirical evidence via simulation (the SIM ablation in Figure 3), rather than a formal proof.

4. The paper shows that removing or rescaling the unbiased rectification step largely fixes the utility drop at low privacy budgets. However, the practical value of the fix is entirely conditional on the generality of the topology domination on utility beyond the tested datasets (Major Issue 1). If topology does not dominate on some other graph, the same fix would not be expected to recover feature-derived utility at low privacy budgets, since the features would carry destroyed information that a rescaling trick cannot restore. If the authors are not able to justify the generality of the topology-dominance claim, then they should make this conditional relationship explicit, so readers are not induced to believe that the rectification fix is a general-purpose solution.

Scientific Contribution
-----------------------
1. Independent Confirmation of Important Results with Limited Prior
   Research
4. Addresses a Long-Known Issue
6. Provides a Valuable Step Forward in an Established Field

Scientific Contribution Comments
--------------------------------
Independent Confirmation with Limited Prior Research:
The paper's finding, that topology-only baselines match or beat feature-based methods on these graphs, confirms facts already established in the graph representation learning literature. This paper provides new, independent confirmation within the LDPGNN setting, an angle with limited prior scrutiny.

Addresses a Long-Known Issue:
The problem of privacy-preserving or otherwise complex learning methods failing to outperform a simple, naive baseline is a long-recognized concern across empirical ML research. This paper exposes a concrete instance of that problem specifically in LDPGNN evaluation, where no prior method had been tested against a feature-free GNN.

Valuable Step Forward in an Established Field:
LDPGNN is a well-established, active research area with several prior methods published at major venues. This paper identifies a previously overlooked gap in how these methods are evaluated, and backs it with both empirical evidence and a mechanistic explanation (the topology-dominance and rectification-amplification findings), offering a methodological correction to the field's evaluation practices.

------------
3. Major but Fixable Flaws in Presentation

Presentation Comments

The paper is well written and well structured. However, its central narrative rests on Technical Major Issue 1 (the unjustified generality of the paper's main claim) holding up under further testing. If this issue cannot be resolved in the authors' favor, for example if the finding does not generalize to non-homophilous graphs, then the paper's abstract, contributions, discussion, and conclusion, which currently state the finding in fully general terms, would need substantial revision, not just added caveats, since the significance and scope of the central claim would change. 

The Technical Minor Issue 4 (the conditional value of the rectification fix) is downstream of this same dependency.

Paper Summary
-------------
Locally private GNNs have users perturb their node features before sending them to a server that already holds the graph, which then rectifies and smooths the reports and trains on them. This paper asks how well does the server do with no features at all? A baseline that just propagates random features over the graph is never beaten by more than 0.79\%, across four datasets, three backbones and privacy budgets from 0.001 to 10, and two ablations trace this to a topology term that dominates the server-side representation and carries no feature information.

Review #906D
===========================================================================

Paper Summary
-------------
This paper investigates the conditions affecting the utility and privacy trade-off in the context of the Locally Differentially Private Graph Neural Networks (LDPGNN). More precisely, the study first proposes a new baseline in which a GNN is trained only on the graph topology without the original node features but rather by assigning random features to nodes. Then, the authors first demonstrate through an empirical evaluation that existing LDPGNN methods usually offer a weaker performance than this baseline showing that actually the graph topology seems to be the main information used by current methods. Afterwards, a theoretical analysis enables to conduct an in-depth analysis on the circumstances under which the current existing LDPGNNs failed compared to the proposed baseline.

Technical Correctness
---------------------
2. Minor Issues

Technical Correctness Comments
------------------------------
The adversary model considered is clearly explained and motivated. The feature-free baseline is cleverly designed and the representation-similarity analysis allows to understand better why LDPGNN current approaches fail to achieve a better performance than the feature-free baseline. More precisely, Theorem 1 clearly shows that the topology-only-term dominates the representation learnt in contrast to other terms. Furthermore, the subsequent analysis and experiments clearly demonstrates that removing the raw-feature information as well as the LDP noise can boost the utility of the feature-free baseline. 

The related work is quite solid but however recent LDPGNN approaches that aims at leveraging the homophily aspect of nodes seems to be missing from the analysis such as the following one called "Leveraging homophile under local differential privacy for effective graph neural networks" from ECML / PKDD 2025 : https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-06096-9_22
Overall, the authors should better justified how they have chosen the three LDPGNN methods thy have analyzed.

Scientific Contribution
-----------------------
3. Creates a New Tool to Enable Future Science
5. Identifies an Impactful Vulnerability
7. Establishes a New Research Direction

Scientific Contribution Comments
--------------------------------
One of the main findings of the investigation is that the baseline proposed called feature-free baseline outperforms most of the LDPGNN approaches for low to high privacy budget, highlighting that most of the information is provided by the structure of the graph itself. The proposed feature-free baseline has been tested on four datasets, three different GNN architectures and compared to four LDPGNN state-of-the-art methods. The results clearly demonstrate that current LDGNN approaches do not seem to be able to effectively used the information provided in the nodes. 

The experiments are accompanied by a theoretical analysis, which clearly explains the reasons behind this behaviour. Some of them are rather counterintuitive such as the fact that the unbiased rectification phase causes a signification degradation of utility under low and medium privacy budget. This has a significant practical implication for the design of LDPGNN methods, highlighting that in contrast to classical LDP mechanisms, such step should be avoided to improve the utility of the downstream GNN classifier. The performance of the UPGNET approach is also re-interpreted with the new analysis, highlighting that actually the denoising phase used in this method seems to improve the encoding of structural information about the graph than really helping to denoise the node features. 

Currently the paper focuses on node privacy but implications for other privacy unit such as edge privacy or the combination of node and edge should be also discussed. In particular, as the topology seems to be the main source of information when training a LDPGNN in the setting considered, it would be interesting to assess how much the perturbation of edge is necessary decrease the success of the feature-free baseline below the performance of state-of-the-art LDPGNN approaches.

Presentation
------------
1. No Flaws in Presentation

Presentation Comments
---------------------
The paper is well-written and the authors have clearly introduced the background on LDP, GNN and LDPGNN. In particular, the main existing LDPGNN methods for achieving node privacy are clearly reviewed. Overall, the paper is very easy to read and the experiments conduced illustrate in an intuitive manner the theoretical analysis.

Comments to Authors
-------------------
The paper provides a very strong result, both through theoretical analysis and experimental validation, demonstrating that current LDPGNN methods are easily outperformed by a simple baseline. This demonstrates that the evaluation of such approaches as well as their design should be clearly reviewed. The main shortcomings of the paper is that it misses some recent LDPGNNs in its analysis, which actually leverage the homophily aspect of the graph to improve on utility, thus potentially achieving a better performance than the feature-free baseline. There is also a very short discussion on the best way to move forward to address the situation, in particular with respect to the design of new LDPGNN approaches.
